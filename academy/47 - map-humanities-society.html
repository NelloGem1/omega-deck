1. WHAT ARE THE HUMAN SCIENCES?

The human sciences (sometimes called human and social sciences, social and behavioral sciences, or knowledge about humanity) comprise every systematic discipline that studies:

  • Human beings as individuals — mind, body, emotion, cognition, development, health.
  • Human beings in groups — families, organizations, markets, states, religions, movements.
  • Human-made systems — laws, economies, technologies, artworks, languages, knowledge itself.
  • The past — history, archaeology, evolutionary prehistory.
  • Meaning — philosophy, ethics, religion, art, literature.

1.1 Why these fields differ from the natural sciences

Natural science asks what is the world made of and how does it work? Human science asks what do humans do, why, and what should we do?. Three consequences:

  1. Reflexivity — humans study themselves; findings change behavior (Goodhart's law, Campbell's law: "the more a measure is used as a target, the more it corrupts the process it was designed to monitor").
  2. Value-ladenness — no human science is value-neutral. Choices of what to measure, which questions to ask, and what to call "progress" embed moral and political commitments.
  3. Path-dependence and contingency — outcomes depend on history, culture, and institutions, not just universal laws. Generalization is statistical and conditional, not absolute.

1.2 The master framework: six interacting layers

Nearly every field in this map can be located along six interlocking layers (expanded in §9):

Layer Question it answers Example fields
Behavior What do individuals actually do? Psychology, behavioral econ, criminology, demography
Institutions What rules and organizations channel behavior? Law, political science, sociology, public administration
Incentives What rewards and punishments motivate action? Economics, game theory, management, education
Culture What shared meanings, values, norms govern life? Anthropology, religious studies, cultural studies, art history
Knowledge How is what we know produced, stored, transmitted? Education, library science, philosophy of science, linguistics
Technology How do tools and systems reshape all the above? HCI, systems science, edtech, media studies

The core thesis of this map: human experience emerges from the feedback loops among these six layers. Technology changes incentives → incentives reshape institutions → institutions reshape culture → culture shapes what counts as knowledge → knowledge designs new technology. No field is complete alone; the richest understanding is transdisciplinary.

2. SOCIAL SCIENCES

The social sciences share a common toolkit: theory building, empirical observation, quantitative and qualitative methods, comparative analysis, and normative evaluation. They differ mainly in unit of analysis: individual (psychology), group (sociology), culture (anthropology), economy (economics), polity (political science), place (geography), population (demography).

2.1 Sociology

What it studies: human social action, groups, institutions, and social structure — from micro-interactions to entire societies and global systems.

Methods: surveys, ethnography, fieldwork, archival and historical analysis, network analysis, experiments (lab, field, natural), regression and causal inference, simulation.

Key thinkers & thought: - Auguste Comte — coined "sociology"; positivism: society can be studied scientifically. - Émile Durkheim — suicide rates as social facts (1897); social integration, anomie, division of labor; founded sociology as an academic discipline. - Max Weber — The Protestant Ethic (1905); verstehen (interpretive understanding); authority types: traditional, charismatic, rational-legal; bureaucracy as iron cage. - Karl Marx — class conflict, modes of production; hugely influential though Marx himself was a philosopher/economist. - George Herbert Mead, Erving Goffman — symbolic interactionism; The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life — we perform roles. - Talcott Parsons, Robert Merton — structural functionalism; manifest vs latent functions; middle-range theory. - C. Wright Mills — The Sociological Imagination (1959): connect personal troubles to public issues. - Pierre Bourdieu — habitus, cultural capital, field theory; how class reproduces through taste and education. - Anthony Giddens — structuration theory: structure and agency co-produce each other. - Elinor Ostrom — Nobel 2009; Governing the Commons: how communities self-organize to manage shared resources without markets or states. - Contemporary: Mustafa Emirbayer (critical realism), Zygmunt Bauman (liquid modernity), Arlie Hochschild (The Managed Heart; emotional labor), Judith Butler (performativity of gender).

Applications: policy evaluation, organizational design, inequality measurement, urban planning, forecasting social movements, understanding polarization and trust.

Connections: psychology (micro-foundations), economics (rational choice & institutional econ), political science (power), anthropology (fieldwork methods), demography (population data).

2.2 Psychology

What it studies: individual mind and behavior — cognition, emotion, motivation, perception, personality, development, social influence, mental health, and the brain-behavior interface.

Methods: controlled experiments, psychometrics (validated scales), longitudinal studies, neuroscience (fMRI, EEG), case studies, meta-analysis, ecological momentary assessment (smartphone sampling).

Key thinkers & thought: - Wilhelm Wundt, William James — founders; structuralism vs functionalism. - Sigmund Freud — psychoanalysis: unconscious, id/ego/superego, defense mechanisms; historically huge, scientifically contested. - Ivan Pavlov, B.F. Skinner — classical and operant conditioning; behaviorism's dominance mid-20th century. - Jean Piaget, Lev Vygotsky — cognitive development; zone of proximal development. - Abraham Maslow — hierarchy of needs (influential, empirically loose). - Carl Rogers, Abraham Maslow — humanistic psychology; self-actualization. - Daniel Kahneman, Amos Tversky — heuristics & biases; prospect theory (Kahneman Nobel 2002); foundation of behavioral economics. - Albert Bandura — social learning theory; self-efficacy; Bobo doll experiments. - Milgram, Zimbardo — conformity & obedience experiments; Stanford prison experiment (now heavily criticized for ethics and validity). - Contemporary: Elizabeth Loftus (false memories), Lisa Feldman Barrett (constructed emotion), Carol Dweck (growth mindset — effect sizes debated), Jonathan Haidt (The Righteous Mind; moral foundations).

Applications: clinical therapy, education design, UX research, public health campaigns, hiring & performance, de-biasing decisions, recommender systems.

Connections: neuroscience (mechanisms), sociology (social context), economics (behavioral econ), education (learning science), HCI (human factors).

2.3 Anthropology

What it studies: humans across all times and places — biological evolution, cultural variation, language, kinship, ritual, power, material culture.

Methods: long-term ethnography (participant-observation), archaeological excavation, comparative method, linguistic analysis, DNA analysis, museum collections.

Key thinkers & thought: - Franz Boas — father of American anthropology; cultural relativism; four-field approach. - Bronisław Malinowski — Argonauts of the Western Pacific (1922); participant-observation method; functionalism. - Margaret Mead — Coming of Age in Samoa; popularizer (later criticized for overstatement); fieldwork on gender & adolescence. - Claude Lévi-Strauss — structural anthropology; binary oppositions; The Savage Mind — "primitive" thought is rigorous logic. - Marcel Mauss — The Gift: reciprocity as foundation of social bonds. - Victor Turner — liminality, communitas; ritual theory. - Mary Douglas — Purity and Danger; pollution as boundary maintenance; grid-group cultural theory. - James C. Scott — Seeing Like a State: legibility and high modernism; Weapons of the Weak. - Contemporary: Arjun Appadurai (scapes), David Graeber (Debt, Bullshit Jobs), Tim Ingold, Philippe Descola.

Applications: UX & product design (cross-cultural research), diplomacy & development, museum curation, policy for indigenous peoples, understanding globalization and migration.

Connections: archaeology (deep past), sociology (social structure), religious studies (ritual), linguistics (language-culture link), economics (gift economies, reciprocity).

2.4 Economics

What it studies: production, distribution, consumption, and exchange of scarce resources — behavior under constraints, markets, incentives, growth, inequality, institutions.

Methods: mathematical modeling, econometrics (natural experiments, instrumental variables, regression discontinuity, RCTs), game theory, agent-based simulation, field experiments, national accounts data.

Key thinkers & thought: - Adam Smith — The Wealth of Nations (1776): division of labor, "invisible hand"; Theory of Moral Sentiments (1759) — sympathy and moral judgment. Often misread as pure selfishness. - David Ricardo — comparative advantage; trade gains. - Karl Marx — labor theory of value, exploitation, crises of capitalism. - John Maynard Keynes — aggregate demand, macroeconomics, General Theory (1936); "in the long run we are all dead." - Friedrich Hayek — The Road to Serfdom; knowledge problem: prices aggregate dispersed local knowledge; Nobel 1974. - Milton Friedman — monetarism, Capitalism and Freedom; methodological individualism ("it's about incentives"). - John Nash, John Harsanyi, Reinhard Selten — game theory Nobel 1994. - Amartya Sen — capabilities approach (freedom, not just income); Nobel 1998; famines as entitlement failures, not food shortages. - Elinor Ostrom — commons governance (with sociology). - Daniel Kahneman & Richard Thaler — behavioral economics (Nobel 2002/2017); nudge theory. - Daron Acemoglu & James Robinson — Why Nations Fail: inclusive vs extractive institutions; Nobel 2024. - Thomas Piketty — Capital in the Twenty-First Century; r > g dynamics. - Contemporary: Esther Duflo (RCT revolution in development, Nobel 2019), Angus Deaton (Nobel 2015), Paul Romer (growth theory), Elhanan Helpman.

Applications: policy (tax, monetary, trade), business strategy, development programs, antitrust, climate economics, predicting crises.

Connections: every social science uses economic reasoning; behavioral econ links to psychology; institutional econ to political science; econometrics to statistics.

2.5 Political Science

What it studies: power, authority, governance, conflict, political behavior, institutions of states and international systems.

Methods: surveys & polls, formal theory & game theory, case studies, comparative-historical analysis, content analysis, experiments (lab & field), elite interviews.

Key thinkers & thought: - Plato & Aristotle — Republic (justice, the philosopher-king); Politics (polity, democracy's dangers). - Niccolò Machiavelli — The Prince (1532): power as it is, not as it should be; realpolitik. - Thomas Hobbes — Leviathan (1651): life without government is "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short"; social contract. - John Locke — natural rights, limited government, consent. - Jean-Jacques Rousseau — general will; Social Contract; Emile on education. - John Stuart Mill — On Liberty: harm principle; representative government. - Max Weber — state monopoly on legitimate violence; authority types (shared with sociology). - Karl Marx, Antonio Gramsci — hegemony: rule through consent + coercion. - Robert Dahl, Joseph Schumpeter — democratic theory; elitism vs pluralism. - Samuel Huntington, Francis Fukuyama — regime stability; "end of history" thesis (contested). - Contemporary: Steven Pinker & others on violence decline; Yascha Mounk on democratic backsliding; political epistemology of misinformation.

Applications: elections & voting design, constitutional design, foreign policy, policy analysis, understanding authoritarianism and democratization.

Connections: law (institutional design), economics (public choice), sociology (power structures), history (state formation).

2.6 Human Geography

What it studies: the spatial organization of human life — cities, regions, migration, economic landscapes, environmental-human interactions, place-making.

Methods: GIS & spatial statistics, remote sensing, demographic modeling, fieldwork, archival cartography, interviews.

Key thinkers & thought: - Humboldt & Ritter — geographic determinism origins. - Paul Vidal de La Blache — possibilism: environment sets limits, culture chooses within them. - Walter Christaller — central place theory; rank-size rule. - David Harvey — Marxist geography; Social Justice and the City; uneven development. - Doreen Massey — power-geometry of space; progressive sense of place. - Edward Soja — thirdspace; postmodern geography. - Yi-Fu Tuan — humanistic geography; sense of place, topophilia. - Contemporary: Mike Davis (City of Quartz, urban dystopias), Neil Brenner (planetary urbanization), mobilities paradigm (John Urry).

Applications: urban planning, disaster response, migration policy, land-use regulation, climate adaptation, retail & logistics siting.

Connections: demography (population maps), sociology (urban sociology), economics (spatial econ), environmental science.

2.7 Demography

What it studies: human populations — size, structure, growth, aging, migration, fertility, mortality, and the social consequences of population change.

Methods: vital statistics, census analysis, cohort-component projection, life tables, fertility surveys, population genetics (indirectly).

Key thinkers & thought: - Thomas Malthus — Essay on Population (1798): population grows geometrically, food arithmetically. - John Maynard Keynes, Simon Kuznets — population and growth links. - Notestein — demographic transition theory: high birth/death → low birth/death. - Amartya Sen — population and entitlements/freedoms. - Hans Rosling — Factfulness: data-driven optimism about global development. - Contemporary: Wolfgang Lutz (population projections), Steven Ruggles (historical demography), IPCC-linked population-climate research.

Applications: pension & healthcare planning, workforce forecasting, school district sizing, climate modeling, migration policy.

Connections: economics (labor markets), sociology (family change), epidemiology (mortality), geography (spatial distribution).

2.8 Behavioral Economics

What it studies: how people actually make economic decisions when psychology meets incentives — systematic deviations from rational choice.

Methods: lab & field experiments, natural experiments, surveys, neuroeconomics, choice architecture audits.

Key thinkers & thought: - Herbert Simon — bounded rationality; satisficing; Nobel 1978. - Daniel Kahneman & Amos Tversky — heuristics & biases; prospect theory; anchoring, availability, representativeness; framing effects. - Richard Thaler — nudge theory, mental accounting, Nudge (with Cass Sunstein, 2008); Nobel 2017. - George Akerlof — market for lemons (information asymmetry); Nobel 2001. - Robert Shiller — narrative economics; Nobel 2013. - Contemporary: Sendhil Mullainathan (scarcity mindset), Anat Rui, Matthew Rabin (reference dependence), Dan Ariely (predictable irrationality experiments).

Applications: tax compliance design, retirement savings (auto-enrollment), organ donation defaults, marketing, fraud prevention, public health messaging.

Connections: psychology (cognitive mechanisms), economics (welfare analysis), public administration (nudge units), ethics (libertarian paternalism debate).

2.9 Criminology

What it studies: crime — its causes, distribution, prevention, policing, punishment, rehabilitation, and the social construction of "criminality."

Methods: official crime statistics, victimization surveys, self-report studies, ethnography, quasi-experiments (hot-spot policing RCTs), life-course analysis.

Key thinkers & thought: - Cesare Beccaria — On Crimes and Punishments (1764): deterrence, proportionality, abolition of torture; founding father of modern penal theory. - Jeremy Bentham — panopticon; utilitarian calculus of punishment. - Émile Durkheim — crime as normal social fact; anomie. - Robert Merton — strain theory: goals vs legitimate means. - Edwin Sutherland — differential association: crime learned through intimate groups. - Travis Hirschi — social bond theory: attachment, commitment, involvement, belief. - Cesare Lombroso — biological positivism (largely discredited; historical cautionary tale). - Labeling theorists (Howard Becker) — Outsiders: deviance is created by social reaction. - Contemporary: Bruce Western (Punishment and Inequality), Todd Clear (social networks & desistance), David Weisburd (hot spots).

Applications: policing strategy, sentencing reform, prison design, restorative justice, community safety, recidivism prediction (with bias risks), gang intervention.

Connections: sociology (inequality, urban decay), law (procedure & rights), psychology (aggression, development), economics (cost of crime, deterrence).

3. HUMANITIES

The humanities study meaning, value, expression, and the human condition — philosophy, history, language, art, religion, literature. They favor interpretation, hermeneutics, critical theory, close reading, archival work, and argument over experiment, though quantitative methods increasingly appear (digital humanities, computational text analysis).

3.1 Philosophy

What it studies: fundamental questions — what exists (metaphysics), what we can know (epistemology), what is right (ethics), how to live (political & practical philosophy), what minds are (philosophy of mind), what science is.

Methods: conceptual analysis, thought experiments, logical argument, historical exegesis, engagement with science, phenomenological description.

Key thinkers & thought: - Socrates — the examined life; dialectic method. - Plato — Forms, the Allegory of the Cave, ideal state; Republic. - Aristotle — virtue ethics, Nicomachean Ethics; teleology; founding logic. - Augustine, Aquinas — Christian synthesis of Greek philosophy and theology. - Descartes — "I think, therefore I am"; mind-body dualism; methodological doubt. - Hume — empiricism, problem of induction, is-ought gap, skepticism. - Kant — categorical imperative; transcendental idealism; Critique of Pure Reason. - Hegel, Marx — dialectic; historical materialism (Marx). - Nietzsche — will to power, perspectivism, critique of morality. - Wittgenstein — language-games; Tractatus and Philosophical Investigations. - Existentialists: Sartre, Camus, Kierkegaard, Heidegger — freedom, absurdity, being-toward-death. - Pragmatists: Peirce, James, Dewey — truth as what works in practice; education reform. - Contemporary: Peter Singer (animal ethics, effective altruism), Martha Nussbaum (capabilities), Judith Butler, Kwame Anthony Appiah (cosmopolitanism), experimental philosophy.

Applications: ethics committees, policy frameworks, technology ethics (AI alignment draws on consequentialism/deontology), legal theory, bioethics, everyday reasoning clarity.

Connections: ethics & law, political philosophy underlies governance, philosophy of science underlies methodology everywhere, cognitive science revisits mind-body.

3.2 Ethics & Moral Philosophy

What it studies: right and wrong, good lives, duties, justice, moral responsibility — applied to medicine, business, technology, animals, the environment.

Methods: normative argument, casuistry, empirical moral psychology, applied case analysis, public deliberation.

Key thinkers & thought: - Utilitarianism: Bentham, Mill — greatest happiness principle; act vs rule utilitarianism. - Deontology: Kant — duties as ends-in-themselves; universalizability. - Virtue ethics: Aristotle — character, phronesis (practical wisdom). - Contractarianism: Hobbes, Rawls — A Theory of Justice (1971): veil of ignorance, difference principle. - Care ethics: Carol Gilligan, Nel Noddings — relationships and attention vs abstract rules. - Peter Singer — Animal Liberation, effective altruism: maximize good per dollar. - Contemporary: effective altruism movement (GiveWell, 80,000 Hours), AI ethics (Bostrom, Russell), longtermism (MacAskill), climate ethics.

Applications: medical ethics, AI governance, business conduct codes, animal welfare law, charitable giving, policy cost-benefit analysis.

Connections: law (rights theory), political science (justice theory), economics (welfare economics), bioethics, technology ethics.

3.3 History

What it studies: the human past — political events, social structures, economies, cultures, ideas, and how the past shapes the present.

Methods: archival research, primary-source criticism, quantitative history (cliometrics), oral history, archaeology integration, comparative history, historiography (history of history-writing).

Key thinkers & thought: - Herodotus & Thucydides — Greek founders; narrative vs analytical history. - Leopold von Ranke — "wie es eigentlich gewesen" — history as it actually happened; source criticism. - Karl Marx — historical materialism; class as engine of history. - Annales School (Bloch, Febvre, Braudel) — longue durée; structures over events. - E.P. Thompson, Eric Hobsbawm — Marxist social history; invention of tradition. - Foucault — Discipline and Punish: power/knowledge; genealogy. - Hayden White — Metahistory: history-writing as rhetoric; narrative emplotment. - Contemporary: Niall Ferguson (controversial syntheses), Yuval Noah Harari (popular, criticized for sweeping claims), digital history & big-data history.

Applications: institutional design (path dependence), policy lessons, cultural memory, museum & heritage work, national identity, anticipating futures.

Connections: archaeology (material evidence), political science (state formation), sociology (social change), economics (cliometrics), memory studies.

3.4 Archaeology

What it studies: the material remains of past human life — settlements, artifacts, bones, landscapes — from prehistory to recent centuries.

Methods: excavation, survey, remote sensing (LiDAR, ground-penetrating radar), radiocarbon & other dating, zooarchaeology, archaeobotany, isotope analysis, aDNA.

Key thinkers & thought: - Christian Jürgensen Thomsen — Three Age System (Stone/Bronze/Iron). - V. Gordon Childe — Man Makes Himself: Neolithic revolution; urban revolution. - Gordon Willey, Lewis Binford — processual archaeology: science over narrative. - Ian Hodder, postprocessualists — meaning, agency, hermeneutics; reflexivity. - Kathleen Kenyon, Mortimer Wheeler — method development. - Contemporary: aDNA revolution (David Reich — Who We Are and How We Got Here), isotope migration studies, climate-archaeology links.

Applications: heritage preservation, indigenous repatriation (NAGPRA), climate-change deep-time analogs, understanding urban origins and inequality.

Connections: history (written + material), anthropology (cultural reconstruction), geology/climate science, museum studies.

3.5 Literature & Literary Studies

What it studies: written and oral texts — novels, poetry, drama, essays — their forms, meanings, contexts, and effects on readers.

Methods: close reading, narrative theory, genre analysis, historical-contextual study, reader-response theory, comparative literature, digital text analysis.

Key thinkers & thought: - Aristotle — Poetics: mimesis, catharsis. - Vladimir Propp — morphology of folktales; structural analysis. - Roland Barthes — The Death of the Author: meaning lives in the reader. - Jacques Derrida — deconstruction; différance. - Michel Foucault — author-function; discourse. - M.M. Bakhtin — dialogism, heteroglossia, carnivalesque. - Edward Said — Orientalism: literature as power/knowledge. - Toni Morrison, Chinua Achebe — postcolonial literary theory. - Contemporary: cognitive narratology, world literature, fan studies, accessibility (plain-language scholarship).

Applications: education (reading comprehension, empathy research), cultural critique, translation, publishing, writing craft, media literacy.

Connections: linguistics (language structure), philosophy (hermeneutics), history (context), cultural studies (power), education.

3.6 Linguistics

What it studies: language — its structure (phonology, morphology, syntax, semantics), acquisition, change, use, diversity, and cognition.

Methods: corpus linguistics, elicitation, fieldwork on endangered languages, psycholinguistic experiments, computational NLP, comparative-historical method.

Key thinkers & thought: - Ferdinand de Saussure — langue vs parole; signs as arbitrary; structural linguistics. - Noam Chomsky — generative grammar; universal grammar; language faculty; deeply controversial empirically. - J.L. Austin, John Searle — speech act theory: saying is doing. - Dell Hymes, William Labov — sociolinguistics; language variation & social class; AAVE recognition. - Benjamin Lee Whorf — linguistic relativity (strong version discredited, weak version supported). - Roman Jakobson, Émile Benveniste — structural & semiotic analysis. - Contemporary: computational linguistics → LLMs; documentary linguistics for endangered languages; creole studies.

Applications: machine translation, education (literacy, bilingualism), forensic linguistics, endangered language preservation, AI alignment via language.

Connections: cognitive science, anthropology (language-culture), education, computer science (NLP), semiotics.

3.7 Religious Studies

What it studies: religions and spiritual traditions — beliefs, rituals, institutions, texts, experiences, ethics — descriptively and comparatively, not theologically.

Methods: textual analysis, ethnographic fieldwork, historical-critical methods, comparative religion, sociology of religion, cognitive science of religion.

Key thinkers & thought: - Max Müller — comparative mythology; "scientific study of religion" founder. - Émile Durkheim — Elementary Forms of Religious Life: religion as social glue. - Sigmund Freud, Karl Marx — religion as illusion/projection vs opium of the people. - Mircea Eliade — sacred vs profane; hierophany (contested universalism). - William James — Varieties of Religious Experience: pragmatist account. - Rudolf Otto — the numinous: mysterium tremendum et fascinans. - Contemporary: Talal Asad (power in religious formations), Talal Asad/Ann Taves (attribution theory of religious experience), cognitive science of religion.

Applications: understanding conflict & peace (religious violence research), healthcare chaplaincy, interfaith diplomacy, museum & heritage, counseling competence.

Connections: anthropology (ritual), sociology (secularization debate), psychology (religious experience), law (religious freedom), history.

3.8 Cultural Studies

What it studies: culture as a site of power — media, popular culture, subcultures, identity, consumption, ideology — how meaning is produced and contested.

Methods: textual/media analysis, ethnography of audiences, discourse analysis, critical theory, reception studies.

Key thinkers & thought: - Stuart Hall — encoding/decoding; articulation theory; race & representation. - Raymond Williams — Keywords; culture as ordinary. - Antonio Gramsci — hegemony (shared with political science). - Michel Foucault, Judith Butler — discourse, performativity, biopower. - Pierre Bourdieu — cultural capital (shared with sociology). - Dick Hebdige — Subculture; style as resistance. - Contemporary: intersectionality (Kimberlé Crenshaw), affect theory, digital culture & platform studies.

Applications: media literacy education, marketing & branding, content moderation policy, public communication, understanding misinformation.

Connections: sociology (culture & stratification), anthropology (ethnography), media studies, political science (ideology), education.

3.9 Art History

What it studies: visual and material art across time and cultures — painting, sculpture, architecture, decorative arts, photography, film — their styles, contexts, meanings, and social functions.

Methods: formal analysis, iconography/iconology, archival research, technical analysis (pigments, provenance), social history of art, reception history.

Key thinkers & thought: - Giorgio Vasari — Lives of the Artists: first art history. - Johann Joachim Winckelmann — neoclassical canon; History of Ancient Art. - Erwin Panofsky — iconology: three levels of meaning. - E.H. Gombrich — The Story of Art; Art and Illusion: perception & schema. - Michael Baxandall — Painting and Experience: social history of the picture. - Linda Nochlin — Why Have There Been No Great Women Artists: structural exclusion. - Contemporary: global art history (de-centering Europe), digital art history, provenance & restitution (Nazi-looted art).

Applications: museum curation, conservation science, authentication & forensics, art market regulation, heritage tourism, design education.

Connections: aesthetics (philosophy), anthropology (material culture), history, conservation science, economics (art markets).

4. ECONOMICS & BUSINESS

Applied human sciences of production, exchange, organization, and value — from markets to firms to money to development.

4.1 Finance

What it studies: allocation of capital over time — investment, risk, returns, markets, banking, monetary systems, financial crises.

Methods: asset pricing models, econometrics, portfolio theory, network analysis of contagion, behavioral finance experiments.

Key thinkers & thought: - Louis Bachelier, Paul Samuelson — efficient markets hypothesis foundations. - Harry Markowitz — portfolio theory (Nobel 1990). - Eugene Fama — efficient market hypothesis; Nobel 2013 (shared). - Robert Shiller — excess volatility; Nobel 2013; Irrational Exuberance. - Daniel Kahneman & Amos Tversky — loss aversion, mental accounting. - Hyman Minsky — financial instability hypothesis: stability breeds instability. - Charles Kindleberger — Manias, Panics, and Crashes: anatomy of bubbles. - Contemporary: behavioral corporate finance, fintech & algorithmic trading, climate-risk finance, crypto debates.

Applications: investment management, banking regulation, risk management, public finance, monetary policy, pension design.

Connections: economics (macroeconomics, monetary theory), psychology (biases), law (securities regulation), systems science (contagion networks).

4.2 Accounting & Auditing

What it studies: measurement and reporting of economic activity — financial statements, costs, budgets, internal controls, assurance.

Methods: double-entry bookkeeping, accrual & cash accounting, auditing standards, forensic accounting, activity-based costing, continuous auditing.

Key thinkers & thought: - Luca Pacioli — Summa (1494): codified double-entry bookkeeping. - Benjamin Graham & David Dodd — Security Analysis: fundamental valuation. - Contemporary: managerial accounting (Kaplan & Norton balanced scorecard), sustainability/ESG reporting (GRI, SASB), forensic accounting after Enron/2008.

Applications: business management, tax compliance, fraud detection, public budgeting, nonprofit accountability, sustainability reporting.

Connections: law (audit regulation), finance, management, information systems, ethics (creative accounting dangers).

4.3 Entrepreneurship & Management

What it studies: how organizations are created, led, structured, innovated, and how firms compete — strategy, operations, HR, organizational behavior.

Methods: case studies, organizational ethnography, experiments, econometric firm-level analysis, agent-based simulation, design thinking.

Key thinkers & thought: - Peter Drucker — The Practice of Management; knowledge worker; management as liberal art. - Chester Barnard — cooperative systems; informal organization. - Elton Mayo, Douglas McGregor — human relations; Theory X/Y (later critiqued as oversimplified). - Michael Porter — five forces; value chain; competitive strategy. - Clay Christensen — disruptive innovation (hypothesis debated). - Gary Hamel, C.K. Prahalad — core competencies. - Richard Florida — creative class (mixed empirical support). - Contemporary: agile/lean methods (Toyota Production System → lean startup, Eric Ries), platform economics (Gawer & Cusumano), behavioral strategy.

Applications: business strategy, startup ecosystems, nonprofit management, public-sector reform, innovation policy.

Connections: economics (industrial organization, contracts), psychology (motivation, teams), sociology (organizations), sociology of work.

4.4 Marketing & Consumer Behavior

What it studies: exchange of value between firms and customers — segmentation, positioning, branding, pricing, channels, persuasion, consumer psychology.

Methods: market research (surveys, experiments, eye-tracking, neuroscience of advertising), big data analytics, A/B testing, ethnography.

Key thinkers & thought: - Philip Kotler — marketing management textbook canon. - E. Jerome McCarthy — 4 Ps framework. - Al Ries & Jack Trout — positioning. - Daniel Kahneman, Dan Ariely — behavioral pricing & choice (anchoring, decoy effects). - Vance Packard — The Hidden Persuaders: critical view of advertising. - Contemporary: digital marketing, influencer economics, dark patterns critique, privacy-preserving measurement (post-cookie era), neuromarketing ethics debates.

Applications: product development, brand strategy, public health campaigns (social marketing), political campaigning, nonprofit fundraising.

Connections: psychology (persuasion, identity), economics (pricing theory), cultural studies (consumer culture), law (advertising regulation, GDPR).

4.5 International Trade & Development Economics

What it studies: exchange across borders — tariffs, trade agreements, comparative advantage, global value chains, poverty, growth, foreign aid, institutions.

Methods: gravity models of trade, trade policy simulation (CGEs), development RCTs, difference-in-differences, instrumental variables (colonial borders, rainfall as instruments).

Key thinkers & thought: - David Ricardo — comparative advantage. - Heckscher-Ohlin — factor endowments model. - Paul Krugman — new trade theory: economies of scale, imperfect competition; Nobel 2008. - Amartya Sen, Joseph Stiglitz, Michael Spence — information economics in development; Nobel 1998/2001. - Daron Acemoglu, James Robinson — extractive vs inclusive institutions; Nobel 2024. - Esther Duflo, Abhijit Banerjee, Michael Kremer — RCT revolution in development; Nobel 2019. - Contemporary: globalization backlash research, supply-chain resilience post-COVID, "deglobalization" debates.

9. HOW THE FIELDS INTERACT

The six layers introduced in §1.2 interact through feedback loops. Key cross-field dynamics:

9.1 Behavior ↔ Institutions

Institutions shape incentives, which shape behavior, which reshapes institutions. Examples: - Property rights (law/economics) → investment incentives → economic growth → pressure for better institutions (Acemoglu & Robinson). - Default rules in contracts (law) → transaction costs → firm structure (Coase/Williamson). - School tracking policies (education/public admin) → self-fulfilling expectations → inequality reproduction (Bourdieu's cultural capital).

9.2 Incentits ↔ Culture

  • Honor cultures & face (anthropology) → different responses to legal sanctions; deterrence fails where shame economy dominates.
  • Protestant ethic thesis (Weber) — disputed, but illustrates culture→economic behavior claims.
  • Performance metrics & Campbell's law — institutionalized incentives corrupt the behaviors they measure (teaching to the test, police quota systems, publication pressure).

9.3 Culture ↔ Knowledge

  • Epistemic communities (Haas) — shared frameworks determine what counts as evidence; Kuhnian paradigms in science.
  • Indigenous knowledge systems (anthropology) — alternative epistemologies; challenges to "universal" science claims; co-production of knowledge movement.
  • Language relativity — language shapes what can be easily thought and said (weak Whorfian effects well-supported).

9.4 Knowledge ↔ Technology

  • Printing press → Reformation → scientific revolution (Eisenstein's argument) — knowledge infrastructure transforms institutions.
  • Search engines & LLMs — externalized cognition changes memory, attention, trust; "Google effect" on recall.
  • Algorithmic curation — recommender systems reshape cultural attention and political information ecosystems.

9.5 Technology ↔ Institutions ↔ Behavior

  • Social media → attention economy → polarization (psychology/political science/platform studies).
  • Fintech → financial inclusion but also new fraud/instability channels.
  • Surveillance technology → chilling effects on behavior; privacy law lagging.

9.6 Cross-cutting recurring themes

  • Power & inequality — nearly every field has a power analysis (Marx, Foucault, Bourdieu, Gramsci, critical race theory).
  • Trust — generalized vs particularized trust predicts economic and institutional performance (Inglehart, Uslaner).
  • Legibility — states and algorithms simplify complex reality; simplification creates blind spots and perverse incentives (Scott's Seeing Like a State).
  • Path dependence — history locks in institutions and technologies (QWERTY, rail gauges, constitutional choices).
  • Reflexivity — social science findings change the systems studied (Lucas critique in economics; Campbell's law generally).

10. HOW THESE FIELDS BUILD BETTER SYSTEMS

10.1 Fairer systems

  • Constitutional design (political science/law): separation of powers, judicial review, federalism as check mechanisms.
  • Anti-discrimination law informed by sociology/psychology evidence on bias.
  • Redistributive policy guided by Sen's capabilities approach vs raw income metrics.
  • Algorithmic fairness research translating social-science bias findings into technical constraints.

10.2 Better education

  • Learning sciences → evidence-based pedagogy: retrieval practice, spaced repetition, formative assessment, cognitive load management.
  • Education economics → returns-to-education data, school funding equity, tracking policy evaluation.
  • EdTech → personalized learning at scale; but see risks in §11.

10.3 Less suffering

  • Effective altruism → cost-effectiveness analysis for charity (GiveWell, malaria nets).
  • Public health + behavioral science → nudges for vaccination, organ donation defaults, hand hygiene.
  • Criminology + restorative justice → alternatives to mass incarceration; recidivism reduction.
  • Development economics RCTs → proven interventions: deworming, cash transfers, bed nets.
  • Psychology/psychiatry → mental health treatment advances; suicide prevention.

10.4 Preserving human experience

  • Archives, museums, oral history → preservation of memory, testimony, cultural heritage.
  • Indigenous knowledge documentation → linguistic & cultural preservation (endangered language archives).
  • Literature, film, music → repositories of human meaning; empathy training through narrative.
  • Digital preservation → OAIS models, format migration, "digital dark age" mitigation.

10.5 Supporting cooperation

  • Game theory → mechanisms for cooperation under self-interest (repeated games, mechanism design).
  • Ostrom's commons governance → community self-organization principles now applied to digital commons (Wikipedia, open source).
  • Diplomacy & international law → institutions that make defection costly and cooperation rewarding.
  • Religious studies & ritual theory → understanding how shared practices create social bonds (Durkheim, Turner).
  • Network science → identifying brokers and bridges that connect disparate groups.

11. RISKS, ETHICS & UNRESOLVED QUESTIONS

11.1 Methodological risks

  • Replication crisis — many psychological & social science findings failed replication (ego depletion, priming, power posing). Effect sizes often overestimated (publication bias, p-hacking).
  • Generalizability (WEIRD problem) — most research uses Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic samples (Henrich).
  • Causal inference difficulty — social systems are reflexive, entangled, historically contingent; RCTs may not generalize (Deaton's "credibility revolution" caveats).
  • Measurement corruption — Campbell's law; Goodhart's law; metric gaming (test scores, police stats, GDP limitations).

11.2 Ethical risks

  • Dual-use — behavioral science techniques enable manipulation: dark patterns, propaganda, addictive design, surveillance capitalism.
  • Surveillance & privacy — digital traces enable unprecedented behavioral monitoring; consent is often illusory (clickwrap).
  • Bias amplification — algorithms trained on historical data reproduce discrimination in hiring, lending, policing, healthcare (COMPAS study).
  • Psychological harm — Milgram/Zimbardo legacy; research ethics (IRB) evolved but global standards uneven.
  • Cultural harm — extractive research on indigenous communities; appropriation of knowledge without benefit-sharing (Nagoya Protocol attempts to address).

11.3 Unresolved questions

  • Nature vs nurture — gene-culture coevolution (richerson & boyd) unresolved balance.
  • How much can social science predict? — forecasting human affairs remains unreliable; Taleb's black swan critique vs Tetlock's superforecasting findings.
  • Consciousness & qualia — philosophy of mind vs neuroscience still divergent.
  • Free will vs determinism — implications for responsibility, law, and ethics unresolved.
  • AI & society — will AGI transform labor, meaning, cooperation? Are we ready institutionally?
  • Sustainability — can economic growth decouple from ecological limits? Degrowth vs green growth debate.
  • Democratic resilience — why do democracies backslide? (Mounk, Levitsky & Ziblatt's institutional forbearance).
  • What makes societies cohesive? — Putnam's social capital decline; loneliness epidemic; trust collapse.

13.3 What this means methodologically

  • Measure decay is now quantified at scale. The corrected nudge literature (d≈0.27→~0 after bias correction) is the social-science analogue of the replication crisis in psychology: headline effects shrink under preregistration, multiplicity correction, and publication-bias adjustment. Any map of human behavior must carry effect-size confidence intervals, not point claims.
  • Polarization is causal, not just descriptive. The 2025 Science reranking experiment is the first large-scale independent demonstration that platform ranking causally moves affective polarization in both directions — shifting the debate from "does social media cause polarization" to "who controls the ranking function and under what mandate."
  • Demography is the slow variable everything else rides on. With ~55% of countries below replacement and several major economies below 1.0, labor scarcity, pension solvency, and immigration politics are structurally locked in for decades — AI exposure (ILO: ~25% of workers) compounds rather than replaces this constraint.
  • Trust is the binding constraint on institutional fixes. Edelman/Pew/GSS convergence — institutional trust at historic lows while interpersonal trust diverges by group — implies reforms that require trust (tax compliance, public health, deliberation) face rising implementation costs.

15.1 Cross-case principles

  • Boundaries enable trust. Every durable institution defines jurisdiction clearly — Ostrom's resource boundaries, ICAC's investigative remit, the marshal badge's lab directory. Ambiguous scope produces either gridlock or capture.
  • Monitoring must be cheap and legible. Ostrom's graduated sanctions work because monitoring is collective and low-cost; NEED_LOG works because logging is automatic. Institutions fail when monitoring costs exceed violation gains' detection probability.
  • Graduated response beats binary punishment. Ostrom's sanction ladder (warning → fine → exclusion) preserves the relationship while deterring; NEED_LOG's FAIL/OK/SELF taxonomy similarly distinguishes error types rather than terminating on first fault.
  • Credibility requires insulation + publicity. ICAC's independence, Singapore's reserves, marshal badges' sealed rosters — all bind future discretion by making deviation visible and costly.
  • Experimentation is an institution. The RCT revolution and the local lab archive share a design: pre-registered hypotheses, reproducible scripts, preserved corrections. Institutions that cannot update on evidence ossify (Tainter, §16).

16.1 How to use cycles honestly

  • Cycles are priors, not predictions. The robust content is structural: demographic transition is locked in; elite-overproduction dynamics recur across empires; complexity carries diminishing returns. The fragile content is timing — no framework predicts when a cycle turns.
  • Distinguish law from metaphor. Demographic transition and Piketty's mechanism have data; Strauss–Howe is literary pattern-matching. This map includes both but labels them.
  • The current moment's cycle position is contested. Turchin's US analysis argues elite overproduction + immiseration resemble pre-collapse historical cases; optimists cite institutional adaptability. The honest reading: the conditions are diagnostic, the outcome is contingent on choices the fields in this map study.
  • AI may be a cycle-disruptor or cycle-accelerant. If AI breaks the labor-productivity link, it could postpone Malthusian constraints (abundance scenario, §17) or accelerate elite-overproduction dynamics (compute/attention concentration). The scenario analysis below treats this as the key uncertainty.

17.5 Synthesis: the five key uncertainties

  1. Will AI compress or widen the meaning-supply of work? — The decisive variable for scenario A vs C is not technical capability but whether institutions (education, labor law, social insurance) adapt faster than displacement. Evidence so far (WEF 2025: ~40% of employers planning reduction; ILO: ~25% exposure) points to task-level transformation before job-level extinction — a window for managed transition.
  2. Does demographic decline force immigration openness or nativist closure? — The 55%-below-replacement statistic is a slow-moving constraint; political systems will face it first in pension/healthcare solvency, then in labor markets.
  3. Can polarization be governed algorithmically? — The 2025 Science experiment proves ranking is a lever; whether societies build accountable levers or entrench engagement-maximizing ones is the open institutional question.
  4. Does trust recover or compound downward? — Edelman/Pew/GSS convergence suggests a ratchet: each institutional failure lowers the baseline for the next fix. Ostrom's finding that local commons can sustain trust where national trust fails is the main counterweight.
  5. Will complexity hit Tainter's wall? — AI + global governance + planetary-scale coordination increase system complexity faster than energy/attention budgets; the cliodynamic question is whether current institutions adapt or ossify.
Bottom line: the 2026 frontier of human science is not a prediction problem but a design problem. Every scenario above resolves through institutional choices — and the case studies of §15 (from Ostrom to the marshal-agent model) show that durable cooperation is built from clear boundaries, cheap monitoring, graduated sanctions, and credible commitment. The fields in this map supply the diagnostic tools; the scenarios supply the stakes.
