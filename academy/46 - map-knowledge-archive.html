⬡ GLOBAL HUMAN KNOWLEDGE AND EXPERIENCE ARCHIVE

Design Specification — knowledge_archive.md

Version 1.0 · Synthesizes the parallel classification maps (math_logic_map.md, ml_pipeline_guide.md, et al.) into one unified architecture.

Mission statement. This is not a Wikipedia clone and not a vector store. It is the institutional memory of human civilization: a permanently maintained, verifiably sourced, cross-disciplinary record of everything humanity has discovered, proved, designed, built, tried, and failed — designed so that the next person (or next civilization) does not repeat the mistakes of the last.

0. DESIGN PHILOSOPHY — why this must exist

0.1 The problem today

Human knowledge is currently scattered, decaying, locked, and unreliable:

Failure mode Real example Cost
Knowledge trapped in heads / proprietary systems Proprietary trade secrets, unpublished lab notebooks Re-derivation of known results; duplicated research
Knowledge lost to media decay War losses, burned libraries (Alexandria, House of Wisdom), endangered oral traditions Entire fields have to be rediscovered
Knowledge frozen in languages few read Science in inaccessible journals, patents in dead formats Global south excluded from frontier knowledge
Knowledge fragmented across silos Physics on arXiv, medicine on PubMed, history in archives — no cross-links Disciplinary blind spots; slow cross-pollination
Failed knowledge deleted Negative results unpublished, failed experiments forgotten Science itself repeats mistakes — the single biggest waste
Knowledge with no provenance Wikipedia citations rot, AI hallucinations fabricate sources Trust collapse

0.2 What "experience" means here

An archive of discoveries without experiences is a museum of answers. The archive must preserve the process: the context, the assumptions that turned out wrong, the resources that failed, the errors that cost lives, the partial successes, the lessons distilled. The failed experiment is first-class knowledge — arguably the most valuable knowledge, because it is the knowledge that prevents the next failure.

A surgeon who knows why the Thalidomide disaster happened will not repeat it. A chemist who knows which esterifications polymerized catastrophically will avoid them. The archive is a negative-knowledge engine as much as a positive-knowledge engine.

0.3 Design principles (the PRIME ladder — foundations first)

1 · 3 · 7 · 9
  • 1 · Provenance first. Every claim carries its source chain. No orphan knowledge enters the canonical layer.
  • 3 · Three pillars. Knowledge content + Knowledge graph + Experience records. All three required; the graph alone is a diagram, the text alone is a pile, the experience alone is a diary.
  • 7 · Seven verification tiers (Section 3). Every node labeled.
  • 9 · Nine imperatives: completeness · verifiability · multilingual · offline-capable · open · uncapturable · auditable · evolving · free.

1. CORE MISSION

1.1 Preserve (the seven knowledge classes)

# Class Examples Why preserved
1 Discoveries laws of physics, new species, historical finds The frontier of the known
2 Mathematics proofs, theorems, algorithms, formal systems The language of reasoning itself
3 Experiences lived histories, oral traditions, field notes Context that papers strip out
4 History events, causality chains, failed states Pattern memory of societies
5 Inventions patents, designs, prototypes, trade secrets (with consent) Applied capability
6 Experiments methods, results, and failures, negative results The scientific method's full record
7 Solutions engineering fixes, medical protocols, legal precedents Reusable resolution patterns

Plus, continuously: culture (art, religion, language, myth), lessons (distilled warnings), designs (architecture, UI, systems).

1.2 The Anti-Repeat-Mistake guarantee

The archive must answer, for any domain, the question: "What has been tried before, and how did it fail?" This requires:

  1. Failure records are mandatory for experiment-type nodes (Section 5).
  2. Failure nodes are cross-linked to the methods they invalidate — a graph edge INVALIDATES(method → prior_result).
  3. Lessons are first-class nodes linked to every failure they distill.
  4. Retrieval must surface failures alongside successes — a search for "fusion reactor design" returns the failed tokamaks with their failure modes before the proposed design (Section 4.6).

1.3 Non-goals

  • Not a replacement for the live literature — an index and memory layer over it.
  • Not an opinion platform — claims require evidence (Section 3).
  • Not a single centralized corporate product — governance is federated (Section 7).

2. KNOWLEDGE HIERARCHY & CLASSIFICATION FRAMEWORK

2.1 The canonical spine

DOMAIN → DISCIPLINE → SUBDISCIPLINE → FIELD → CONCEPT → PRINCIPLE →
METHOD → EXPERIMENT → EVIDENCE → APPLICATION → OUTCOME

This is a containment + derivation hierarchy, not just a taxonomy. Each level has a defined semantics:

Level Definition Example (energy domain)
Domain Top-level human-knowledge area Domain: Natural Sciences
Discipline Recognized academic/professional field Discipline: Physics
Subdiscipline Major branch Subdiscipline: Plasma Physics
Field Specific research/practice area Field: Magnetic Confinement Fusion
Concept Atomic idea/entity Concept: Tokamak
Principle Generalized law/pattern Principle: Lawson Criterion
Method Reproducible procedure Method: Neutral Beam Injection
Experiment A specific attempt with context Experiment: JET D-T campaign 1997
Evidence Observation/datum supporting a claim Evidence: JET achieved 16 MW fusion power
Application Deployed use Application: ITER design basis
Outcome Measured result / status Outcome: Q=0.67 achieved; gain not yet >1

2.2 Cross-cutting axes (orthogonal to the spine)

The spine is one axis. Every node also carries:

  • Epistemic tier (Section 3) — how confident we are.
  • Knowledge class (Section 1.1) — what kind of knowledge it is.
  • Time axis — discovered_at, superseded_by, valid_from/until.
  • Space/culture axis — region, tradition, language of origin.
  • Confidence & controversy flags — disputed, consensus, fringe.

2.3 The classification maps unify here

The parallel maps (math_logic_map.md covers math/logic; ml_pipeline_guide.md covers ML pipelines) plug into this spine at specific levels:

  • math_logic_map → Domain: Formal Sciences → Discipline: Mathematics / Logic / Computer Science → ... → Concept: e.g. Gödel Incompleteness → Principle: ... → Method: proof technique → Experiment: formal verification.
  • ml_pipeline_guide → Domain: Applied Sciences → Discipline: Computer Science → Subdiscipline: Machine Learning → Field: e.g. RAG pipelines → Concept: embedding → Principle: ... → Method: fine-tuning → Experiment: eval run → Evidence: benchmark scores → Application: deployed model → Outcome: measured quality.

New maps (biology, history, medicine, law, arts) slot in identically. The spine is domain-agnostic by design.

Temporal / provenance:

PUBLISHED_IN     (document → venue/journal)

CONDUCTED_BY     (experiment → team/org)
USES             (experiment → method/tool/dataset)
CITES            (document → document)

Social / cultural:

TEACHES          (person → concept/lesson)
PRACTICED_IN     (tradition → region)
RELATED_TO       (weak semantic link, high fan-out — used sparingly)

Every edge carries: weight (confidence 0..1), source (which evidence chain justifies the edge), valid_from, valid_until, created_by, verification_tier.

2.5 Open questions as nodes

Unsolved problems are n:Question nodes — "What is dark matter?", "P vs NP?", "How did the Indus script work?". They link OPEN edges to n:Hypothesis nodes (candidate answers) and BLOCKED_BY edges to n:Failure/n:Limitation nodes. Gap detection (Section 4.7) is partly the automated discovery of under-connected n:Question nodes.

3. VERIFICATION FRAMEWORK

3.1 The nine epistemic tiers

Every claim node carries exactly one epistemic_tier. Ordered strongest → weakest:

Tier Name Definition Example
T1 Verified Fact Independently reproduced, consensus, no credible dispute Water boils at 100°C at 1 atm
T2 Math Proof Formal proof, machine-checkable (Lean/Coq) or accepted √2 is irrational
T3 Theory Well-supported explanatory framework, not directly observable Evolution by natural selection
T4 Observation Direct measurement/observation, possibly unrepeated A supernova sighting
T5 Expert Opinion Credible authority's judgment, no formal proof A surgeon's protocol recommendation
T6 Personal Experience First-hand account, unverifiable by others "This bridge design failed in my project"
T7 Hypothesis Testable conjecture, awaiting evidence A proposed dark-matter candidate
T8 Unverified Claim with weak/absent provenance Forum post, hearsay
T9 Speculation Opinion/philosophy without empirical anchor "AI will solve climate change"

Rules: - Tiers are per-claim, not per-document. A paper can contain T2 math and T8 speculation; each proposition gets its own node. - Tiers can be promoted (T7 → T3 after evidence accumulates) or demoted (T3 → T7 if contradicted). Promotion/demotion is logged. - T6 (personal experience) is never deleted but is visually and algorithmically separated from T1–T5 in retrieval — a search can request "only verified" or "include experiences."

3.2 Source verification pipeline

Every ingested source passes through:

INGEST → AUTHORSHIP CHECK → PROVENANCE CHAIN → EVIDENCE EVAL →
TIER ASSIGNMENT → CROSS-CHECK → CANONICAL INDEX
  • Authorship check: is the claimed author real? (ORCID, institutional affiliation, publication record). Detects impersonation and sockpuppetry.
  • Provenance chain: document → raw data → experimental apparatus → raw observations → processed results → published claim. Broken chain = tier capped at T8.
  • Evidence evaluation: for each n:Evidence node, record replication_count, independent_replications, retraction_status, funding_conflicts, sample_size, methodology_rigor_score.

3.3 Contradiction detection

Contradictions are features, not bugs. The archive stores them:

  • CONTRADICTS edges link incompatible claims.
  • Contradiction clusters are n:Controversy nodes that aggregate mutually-contradicting claims (e.g., "climate sensitivity range").
  • Each controversy node has a resolution_status: open, resolved_for_X, unresolved, resolved_by_consensus, resolved_by_experiment.
  • Contradictions are surfaced in retrieval: a query about a contested topic returns both sides plus the controversy node, never silently picks one.

3.4 Outdated-knowledge correction

Knowledge decays. The archive handles it via:

  • Temporal validity: valid_from/valid_until on claims.
  • Supersession chains: SUPERSEDES edges; the old node is never deleted but is marked superseded_by and demoted.
  • Deprecation events: when a field overturns (e.g., a drug recalled, a cosmology model revised), a DeprecationEvent cascades: demote all dependent claims by one tier, flag for re-review.
  • Re-verification schedule: high-impact claims (medical, safety, structural) get periodic re-verification triggers. Stale claims decay in ranking.

3.5 Uncertainty representation

Every node carries a structured uncertainty object:

uncertainty:
  epistemic_tier: T4
  confidence: 0.72            # 0..1 aggregate
  known_unknowns:             # what we don't know
    - "exact replication count beyond 3"
  contested_by: [n:Controversy#climate-sensitivity]
  last_verified: 2024-06-01
  decay_half_life_days: 1825  # how fast this claim ages

The AI engine (Section 4) must surface uncertainty in every answer — an answer without an uncertainty statement is a bug, not a feature.

4. AI KNOWLEDGE ENGINE

4.1 Capabilities (the ten)

# Capability Mechanism
1 Semantic search Dense vector retrieval over chunked nodes + BM25 hybrid
2 Graph reasoning Cypher/GQL traversals over the knowledge graph
3 RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) Graph-grounded retrieval → LLM synthesis with citations
4 NLU (Natural Language Understanding) Entity/relation extraction from text into graph nodes
5 Cross-disciplinary discovery Graph paths connecting distant domains (Section 4.5)
6 Pattern recognition Graph motif detection, embedding clustering
7 Hypothesis generation LLM + graph completion: propose edges/nodes filling gaps
8 Gap detection Under-connected questions, missing evidence, contradictions
9 Outcome learning Track which retrieved answers were useful/correct → rerank
10 Provenance tracing Every AI answer cites node IDs → full evidence chain

4.2 Retrieval architecture (hybrid, four channels)

Query
 ├─→ [C1] Dense vector search      (semantic similarity, pgvector/Qdrant/LanceDB)
 ├─→ [C2] Sparse BM25               (keyword, Postgres FTS / Elasticsearch)
 ├─→ [C3] Graph traversal           (Cypher: expand from seed entities)
 └─→ [C4] Structured filter         (epistemic_tier, domain, date, language)
        │
        ▼
   Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF)
        │
        ▼
   Cross-encoder reranker (optional, for high-stakes queries)
        │
        ▼
   Context assembly → LLM synthesis → answer + citations + uncertainty

4.3 Graph-RAG pattern (the key innovation)

Standard RAG retrieves text chunks. Graph-RAG retrieves structured knowledge neighborhoods:

  1. Extract entities from the query ("tokamak", "fusion gain").
  2. Find seed nodes in the graph.
  3. Traverse k hops collecting: the node, its n:Evidence, its n:Experiments, its n:Failures, its n:Lessons, its SUPERSEDES/CONTRADICTS neighbors.
  4. Serialize the subgraph into a prompt with node IDs as citation anchors.
  5. LLM answers grounded in the subgraph, citing node IDs.

This is the difference between "what does the internet say about tokamaks" and "here are the 7 documented tokamak failure modes, each with its experiment ID and lesson node."

4.4 Anti-hallucination contract (mandatory)

Every AI-generated answer must satisfy:

  1. Citation requirement: every factual claim in the answer maps to ≥1 node ID that exists in the graph and is retrievable. Uncited claims are stripped or marked [UNSOURCED].
  2. Evidence floor: answers to empirical questions must cite n:Evidence nodes; pure-LLM generation is forbidden for empirical claims.
  3. Uncertainty disclosure: every answer ends with the aggregate uncertainty of its cited nodes (lowest tier wins).
  4. Tier respect: the engine never presents T6/T8/T9 material as T1–T3. Experience reports are labeled "personal account — not independently verified."
  5. Refusal: if retrieval returns no supporting nodes, the engine says "the archive does not contain verified knowledge on this" — it does not confabulate.
  6. Adversarial check: generated answers are re-verified against the source nodes before display (a second pass checking that citations actually support the claims).

4.5 Cross-disciplinary discovery

The graph enables serendipitous retrieval: paths between distant domains.

Example mechanism — analogical bridge search: - Query: "how to improve battery thermal management" - Graph finds n:Concept:Phase-Change Material in Domain: Materials Science - Finds n:Concept:Regenerative Cooling in Domain: Aerospace Engineering - Finds shared n:Principle:Latent Heat Absorption - Surfaces the aerospace method as a candidate for batteries → generates a n:Hypothesis node for human review.

Implementation: weighted shortest-path / random-walk-with-restart over the graph, seeded from query entities, filtered by epistemic_tier ≤ T5, scored by path novelty (penalize paths within same discipline).

4.6 Failure-first retrieval (the killer feature)

For any n:Method or n:Experiment query, retrieval always includes: - All n:Failure nodes connected via INVALIDATES or sharing the method. - All n:Lesson nodes distilled from those failures. - A "known failure modes" summary block at the top of the answer.

A user designing a bridge gets the collapse records first. This is the archive's reason to exist.

4.7 Gap detection

Automated processes that find holes in human knowledge:

  • Under-connected questions: n:Question nodes with no n:Hypothesis children, or hypotheses with no n:Experiment children.
  • Evidence deserts: n:Principle nodes with claims but zero n:Evidence.
  • Contradiction clusters with no active n:Experiment attempting resolution.
  • Temporal gaps: fields with no new evidence in >N years.
  • Cross-domain gaps: principles proven in domain A with no attempted application in analogous domain B.

Gap reports are published as n:Report nodes — a continuously updated map of what humanity doesn't know, which is itself valuable knowledge.

4.8 Outcome learning

Every AI answer interaction is logged (privacy-preserving, Section 7): - Was the answer useful? (thumbs, follow-up query, citation click-through) - Was a cited node later found wrong? (feeds back to demotion) - Did the user act on a n:Lesson and avoid a failure? (the holy grail — tracked via optional "I used this" reports)

This closes the loop: the archive learns which knowledge prevents harm.

5. EXPERIENCE RECORD SCHEMA

5.1 The canonical record

Every significant human experience — a failed experiment, a field deployment, a medical case, a historical event witnessed, a design iteration — is stored as an Experience Record with this mandatory structure:

experience_record:
  id: exp:uuid
  title: "J-TEXT-0042: Li-S battery thermal runaway in pouch cells"
  epistemic_tier: T6              # personal experience — never auto-promoted
  class: experiment_failure      # one of the 7 classes + experience subtypes

  # --- the spine ---
  problem: "Pouch-cell Li-S batteries overheated during fast-charge cycling"
  context:
    when: "2023-2024"
    where: "lab, Shanghai"
    conditions: "25°C ambient, 2C charge rate, 80% SOC window"
    participants: [person:uuid, org:uuid]
    prior_state: "cells rated for 500 cycles"
  objective: "Achieve 1000 cycles at 2C without capacity fade >20%"

  # --- the attempt ---
  assumptions:
    - "Electrolyte stable to 60°C"
    - "Separator prevents dendrite propagation"
    - "BMS cutoff at 55°C sufficient"
  actions:
    - step: 1
      action: "Charged 20 cells at 2C to 80% SOC"
      tool: "Neware BTS4000 cycler"
      resource_cost: "480 cell-hours"
    - step: 2
      action: "Accelerated aging at 45°C"
      tool: "thermal chamber"
      resource_cost: "720 cell-hours"
  resources:
    materials: ["Li-S pouch cells ×20", "LiTFSI electrolyte"]
    equipment: ["BTS4000", "thermal chamber", "EIS analyzer"]
    funding: "internal R&D"
    personnel_hours: 340

  # --- the outcome ---
  results:
    quantitative:
      - metric: "cycle life"
        expected: ">1000 cycles"
        actual: "mean 312 cycles, σ=47"
      - metric: "failure mode"
        actual: "thermal runaway at cycle ~300, venting + ignition"
    qualitative: "cells swelled after cycle 250; EIS showed SEI growth"
  failures:
    - what_failed: "SEI layer grew exponentially above 40°C"
      when_detected: "cycle 180 (post-mortem EIS)"
      root_cause_hypothesis: "electrolyte oxidation at high temp + high rate"
      could_have_been_predicted_by: ["accelerated aging test at higher temp earlier"]
  solutions_attempted:
    - "reduced charge rate to 1C → extended life to 480 cycles (still insufficient)"
    - "added ceramic separator → delayed but did not prevent runaway"
  solutions_that_worked: []        # empty = unresolved
  evidence:
    - type: "raw_data"
      location: "dataset:uuid (cyclers logs, EIS spectra)"
      reproducible: true
    - type: "images"
      location: "artifact:uuid (post-mortem photos)"
    - type: "third_party"
      location: "paper:uuid (confirms similar failure in literature)"
  lessons:
    - "Li-S pouch cells need active cooling above 1C charge rates"
    - "SEI growth is the leading indicator — monitor EIS from cycle 50"
    - "Accelerated aging at 45°C predicts 25°C failure ~3x faster"
  knowledge_links:
    methods_used: [method:uuid]
    principles_violated: [principle:lawson-criterion-analogue]
    related_failures: [exp:uuid (similar Li-ion thermal events)]
    contradicts: []
    supports: [hypothesis:uuid]

  # --- provenance ---
  author: person:uuid
  organization: org:uuid
  recorded_at: 2024-11-25
  verification:
    independently_replicated: false
    replication_status: "single-lab"
    peer_reviewed: false
    raw_data_available: true

5.3 Failed experiments as first-class citizens

The schema requires the failures: block for any class: experiment* record. A failure record with empty failures is rejected at ingestion — this is the archive's core invariant.

Failed experiments get special retrieval treatment: they are indexed under their root_cause and lessons, not just their objective, so a search for "thermal runaway causes" finds them even if the user never searched for "battery cycling."

5.4 Promotion path for experiences

A personal experience can gain epistemic weight:

T6 (personal) → T4 (observation) → T3 (theory) → T1 (verified fact)
     │                │                │              │
     │                │                │              └─ consensus + replication
     │                │                └─ pattern across many experiences
     │                └─ independently replicated experiment
     └─ initial recording (always T6 or lower)

Promotion requires: independent replication, peer review, or convergence with existing verified knowledge. Never automatic.

6.6 Distributed storage strategy

Phase 1 (prototype): single Postgres + Qdrant + Neo4j on one machine. Phase 2 (national/regional): PostgreSQL read replicas, Qdrant cluster, Neo4j causal cluster, S3 object storage, CDN. Phase 3 (global):

  • Sharding by domain: each discipline cluster owns its subgraph; cross-domain links via a federated query layer.
  • IPFS/Filecoin for immutable document storage — content-addressed, no single point of failure, censorship-resistant.
  • Byzantine fault-tolerant replication of the audit log [SPEC].
  • Merkle-tree integrity: every node's content hash chains into a global Merkle root, periodically anchored to a public blockchain (or a public transparency log like Certificate Transparency) for tamper-evident timestamps [EXISTS: CT logs; SPEC: full BFT consensus].

6.7 Crypto integrity model

Every node:
  content_hash = SHA-256(canonical_serialization(node))
  signature   = Ed25519_sign(content_hash, author_private_key)

Every edge:
  edge_hash   = SHA-256(src_id + dst_id + edge_type + weight + timestamp)

Audit log:
  record_hash = SHA-256(prev_hash + payload + timestamp + signature)
  → tamper-evident chain; any modification breaks all subsequent hashes

Global state:
  merkle_root = Merkle(root of all node_hashes)
  → anchored externally (blockchain / transparency log) every N hours
  → anyone can verify any node's inclusion without trusting the archive

This gives verifiable integrity without trusting the operator — the archive can prove "this claim existed on date X with this content" even if the operator is compromised.

Access is granted by reputation + community endorsement, not by payment. No pay-to-edit. No pay-to-verify.

7.3 Reputation system

  • Every contribution starts with reputation_delta = 0.
  • Endorsements from VERIFIED users: +1 to +5 (weighted by endorser reputation).
  • Successful verification of your contribution: +10.
  • Contribution later found wrong/retracted: -20.
  • Vandalism/spam: -50 + suspension.
  • Reputation decays over time (half-life ~180 days) to prevent entrenchment.
  • Reputation gates access tiers but never overrides epistemic tier — a high-reputation user can still only submit T8 claims that must be verified.

7.4 Transparency & auditability

  • All writes are logged in the hash-chained audit log.
  • All tier changes are logged with reason and evidence.
  • All AI answers are logged (privacy-preserving: query hash, not raw query, unless user opts in) for outcome learning and abuse detection.
  • Public audit endpoint: anyone can verify the Merkle root and check inclusion proofs for any node.
  • Open governance meetings: protocol changes proposed as n:Proposal nodes, debated publicly, ratified by stakeholder vote.

7.5 Correction protocol

When an error is found:

1. Anyone can file a Dispute (POST /dispute) with evidence.
2. Dispute creates a n:Controversy node linking the disputed claim
   and the counter-evidence.
3. Affected claim is demoted one tier pending review.
4. Reviewers (VERIFIED+) examine evidence, may:
   a. Uphold original → controversy resolved, tier restored
   b. Accept correction → node updated, SUPERSEDES edge created,
      old version preserved in history
   c. Escalate → GOVERNANCE-level review
5. All steps are in the audit log. The correction itself is a knowledge
   node — "we were wrong about X, here's how we found out."

Corrections are celebrated, not hidden. A correction is proof the system works. The archive's credibility depends on visible self-correction.

11.1.1 What the local ledger changes in the design

  1. SQLite is not a toy when scoped. fleet.db proves that a single-file database can index agents, experiments, results, bugs, docs, and badges with enough fidelity for search and audit. The archive’s Phase-0 SQLite/LanceDB plan is therefore not speculative; it is already exercised.
  2. Results need source pointers. Every result row carries a source path (science_lab/.../results*.json, CSV, or report). The archive should make source_artifact mandatory, not optional.
  3. Failures must be rows, not prose. The bugs table was populated by regexing AUTOPSY.md/CODE_AUTOPSY.md. That is brittle but useful; the canonical schema should instead require structured bug records with file, line, symptom, root cause, fix, and verification status.
  4. Replication is a feature and a hazard. The vault sync triplicated many scripts, preserving recoverability but also freezing bugs. The archive needs drift detection: identical copies are redundancy until one copy is patched; then they become divergent evidence.
  5. Credentials need reconciliation. Badge counts differ across presentation, roster, and database layers. The archive should publish reconciliation reports whenever derived indexes disagree with source registries.
  6. Autopsy is a knowledge class. A postmortem is not merely debugging; it is a negative-experience record with process anatomy, resource metabolism, reproduction steps, and lessons. It belongs in Section 5 as postmortem/code_autopsy.

11.3 Forgetting-curve and memory-science frontier

Spaced repetition is not merely a study hack; it is the empirical science of knowledge decay. For an archive, forgetting curves explain why verification tiers, re-verification schedules, and outcome learning cannot be static fields.

FindingEvidence anchorArchive interpretation
Forgetting is not one universal exponential. Kornell & Bjork (2025) show empirical forgetting curves are influenced by the distribution of memory strengths relative to a recall threshold; a distribution model explains why curves can look linear or concave when percent correct is high. Memories “just above threshold” help short-term tests but do not necessarily form lasting memory. Claims should not have one global decay_half_life. Half-life should depend on domain, claim type, evidence strength, usage, and user/community memory state.
FSRS reframes scheduling as prediction. FSRS models per-card difficulty, stability (time for recall probability to fall from 100% to 90%), and retrievability. Anki 25.07 shipped FSRS-6; the open benchmark reports ~727 million reviews from ~10k users and ~350 million evaluation reviews. FSRS-7 uses fractional intervals and a more complex curve. The archive can schedule verification audits like reviews: high-impact claims return before confidence decays below threshold; low-value claims age out of prominence without deletion.
Power-function fits beat naive exponential assumptions. FSRS technical documentation describes movement from exponential to power-function retrievability curves because superposed exponentials are better approximated by power functions. Use flexible decay models, not a single Ebbinghaus-style curve. Store model version and parameters so decay predictions remain auditable.
Spacing, testing, and desirable difficulty remain core. The same literature and FSRS ecosystem imply that retrieval practice strengthens memory; for archives, retrieval should be treated as an active maintenance operation, not passive search. “Outcome learning” (§4.8) should include spaced re-querying, citation click-through, correction events, and “used this lesson” reports as memory-strength signals.

Boundary. FSRS predicts an individual learner’s recall probability. The archive must not pretend an institutional claim “forgets” like a flashcard. The correct transfer is the scheduling principle: review before predicted confidence decay, weight review urgency by consequence, and log every intervention.

Worked example. Suppose a query has three relevant failure records. Top-10 returns all three, first at rank 2, plus seven irrelevant items: Recall@10 = 3/3 = 1.0, Precision@10 = 3/10 = 0.3, MRR = 1/2 = 0.5. For the archive, recall of the failure set matters more than precision cosmetics; the seven irrelevant items should be demoted, but no failure record may be dropped.

Production rule. Evaluate retrieval before generation. If the decisive evidence is not in context, the LLM cannot reliably cite it. Separate search problems from prompting problems; tune for recall on failure queries and precision on high-consequence factual queries.
